Why the curves look the way they do

The energy landscape — where the snap actually comes from

This is the conceptual view: forget tube diameters for a moment and look at the shape of the energy function. The ball sits in a valley of the potential V(θ) = ½(θ − α)² − λ cos θ. The first term is a spring pulling the tube toward whatever angle the motor commands (α); the second is the two tubes trying to align with each other. Turn the motor and the whole landscape tilts. Past a critical tilt the valley flattens out and disappears — and the ball has nowhere to go but roll to the next valley. That roll is the snap.
Bifurcation parameter λ2.80
In this one-variable caricature the fold appears at λ > 1 (where 1 + λ cos θ can go negative). The π²/4 ≈ 2.47 threshold you see quoted belongs to the continuous rod problem on the other tab — same mechanism, different number, because there θ varies along the length.

Reading the fold

Below λ = 1 the S-curve isn't S-shaped at all — it's a single-valued line. Every motor angle maps to exactly one tip angle, the tube tracks smoothly, and there is nothing to snap. Drag λ below 1 and watch the dashed middle branch vanish. (Careful: this threshold is 1 for this one-variable model, not the π²/4 ≈ 2.47 quoted for the full rod — the mechanism is identical, but the critical number depends on how many degrees of freedom you keep.)

Above it, the curve folds over onto itself: for a range of motor angles there are three equilibria — two stable, one unstable between them. The tube is sitting on the upper or lower stable branch. As you keep turning, your branch curves around toward the fold point, where it merges with the unstable branch and both annihilate. That is the same double condition from the math: V′(θ) = 0 and V″(θ) = 0 at once. The valley hasn't just gotten shallow, it has ceased to exist.

This is why snapping is hysteretic. Sweep α up and it snaps at one angle; sweep back down and it snaps at a different one. The tube's state depends on where it has been, not just where the motor is now — which is exactly what produces the staircase plot on the design tab, and why the solver there has to track branches rather than just solve for a root.

How this relates to the other tab. This landscape is the intuition-level caricature: one number θ, one spring, one well. The design tab solves the real thing — θ(s) varying continuously along the tube, governed by θ″(s) = (λ/Lc²) sin θ(s), with your actual diameters, curvatures and lengths feeding into λ. Same bifurcation, same fold, infinitely many degrees of freedom instead of one.

Model: two-curved-member elastic bifurcation theory (Gilbert, Hendrick & Webster, IEEE T-RO 2016), solved here as a shooting-method boundary value problem θ''(s) = (λ/Lc²) sin θ(s) with Newton continuation to track the loaded branch through fold (snap) points. Hydrodynamic and motor-energy figures are first-order quasi-steady estimates layered on top for design intuition — validate against your own test jig before committing to a build.

This model is frictionless. Tube-on-tube sliding and twisting contact is not represented anywhere above — the formulation is purely conservative elastic energy. Gilbert et al. list friction as a leading unmodeled error source, and note that every one of their 45 experimental trials snapped at a larger base angle than the model predicted. Expect the same bias here: real snaps land later, need more torque to trigger, and release less energy into the water than shown, because some is lost to contact heating and some never gets stored in the first place. Treat all energy and thrust numbers as optimistic upper bounds.
